\title{Rejecting Chaser-Side Base-Attitude Disturbances during Contact Detumbling
of Non-Cooperative Debris with a Free-Floating Space Manipulator}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Karam Khasawneh \textit{et al.}}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Dept.\ of Robotics and Artificial Intelligence}\\
\textit{Jadara University}, Irbid, Jordan\\
KaramQ5@ieee.org}}

\begin{abstract}
Capturing and detumbling non-cooperative space debris is a defining challenge for on-orbit
servicing: the target tumbles, its mass and inertia are unknown, and the chaser is a
\emph{free-floating} platform whose own attitude is disturbed both by every arm motion and by
unmodeled actuation noise (e.g.\ attitude-control-system and thruster transients). Prior
reinforcement-learning (RL) work for space manipulators concentrates on the pre-contact phase
(reaching and tracking a grasp point), where base disturbance is minimized \emph{before} contact;
post-grasp detumbling is handled by model-based force-feedback controllers such as active
resistance control (ARC), which are inertia-agnostic by design but carry no term that responds to
a disturbance originating at the \emph{chaser's own base} during contact. We address this gap with
an RL controller for the post-grasp detumbling phase that is trained to \emph{reject a chaser-side
base-attitude disturbance} while detumbling the target, observing kinematics only (no contact-force
sensor). In a MuJoCo free-floating-base simulation with full contact dynamics, evaluated against a
re-implemented ARC baseline on a paired sweep, the learned policy holds peak induced base-attitude
disturbance flat and low as the injected disturbance grows, whereas ARC lets it rise to the
open-loop level, at matched detumbling performance and target-inertia parity over
$0.5\times$--$2\times$ nominal. The learned policy reduces peak induced base-attitude disturbance by
$\sim 80\%$ ($\approx 5\times$) relative to ARC across the $0.05$--$0.12$ rad/s range, with the gap
widening to $6.4\times$ at $2\times$ nominal inertia, using kinematic observations only.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Active debris removal and on-orbit servicing increasingly require a chaser spacecraft to capture a
\emph{non-cooperative}, tumbling target with a robotic arm and bring its spin to rest. The problem
decomposes into reaching a grasp point, establishing contact, and \emph{detumbling} the now-coupled
target. A defining feature of the setting is that the chaser is \emph{free-floating}: with no fixed
base, momentum is conserved, so every arm motion reacts back onto the base attitude. During contact
this coupling is compounded by disturbances originating at the chaser itself --- attitude-control
thruster transients, solar-array flexing, and other unmodeled actuation noise --- that perturb the
base while the arm is fighting the target's spin.

The reinforcement-learning (RL) literature for space manipulators has concentrated on the
\emph{pre-contact} phase: a free-floating arm tracks a capture point while minimizing base
disturbance, but only before contact dynamics are engaged \cite{guidance2510,wei2025robotica}. The
post-grasp detumbling phase is instead handled by model-based controllers, of which active
resistance control (ARC) \cite{activeresist2025} is a strong representative: it tracks contact force
and, by design, requires no knowledge of the target's mass or inertia. ARC's control law, however,
contains no term that responds to a disturbance acting on the \emph{chaser's own base}; it tracks
contact force only. Pre-contact RL rejects base disturbance before contact; ARC ignores it during
contact. The rejection of a \emph{chaser-side} base-attitude disturbance \emph{during} contact
detumbling is therefore an open gap --- and, unlike target-side inertia robustness (which ARC
already provides), a genuinely uncontested one.

We address that gap with a learned detumbling controller and benchmark it against a re-implemented
ARC baseline in a MuJoCo free-floating-base simulation with full contact dynamics. Our
contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item An RL formulation of \emph{post-grasp detumbling} on a free-floating base with contact
      dynamics, trained to reject an injected chaser-side base-attitude disturbance (a proxy for
      ACS/thruster noise) while detumbling the target.
\item A paired benchmark against ARC --- correctly characterized as inertia-agnostic --- on
      base-attitude disturbance (chaser ACS-fuel proxy), spin reduction, and contact force, showing
      the learned policy rejects the disturbance where ARC's force-tracking law does not.
\item An ablation that removes the base-attitude penalty, isolating it as the mechanism responsible
      for rejection; and an inertia-parity check confirming the advantage is not an artifact of
      target-inertia mismatch.
\item A sensing-reduction result: the policy observes kinematics only, with no contact-force sensor,
      yet matches ARC's detumbling and beats it on base disturbance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
We organize prior work into three clusters and position our contribution against the gap they
jointly leave open.

\subsection{High-level mission planning}
Selecting and sequencing \emph{which} debris to visit, and in what orbit-transfer order --- via
reinforcement learning, Monte-Carlo tree search, or operations-research solvers --- is orthogonal to
the closed-loop control problem we study and belongs to a companion line of work. We mention it only
as mission context and do not benchmark against it.

\subsection{Pre-contact RL guidance and tracking}
The bulk of RL research for space manipulators targets the \emph{pre-contact} phase: a free-floating
arm reaches and tracks a capture point on a tumbling target while minimizing the base disturbance
induced by arm--base dynamic coupling and avoiding self-collision. Representative examples are
TD3-based obstacle-free guidance for a free-floating manipulator \cite{guidance2510} and deep-RL
trajectory planning for non-cooperative tumbling-target capture \cite{wei2025robotica}, the latter
penalizing base-pose disturbance directly in its reward. This phase has even reached hardware: Hovell
and Ulrich demonstrate deep-RL-guided spacecraft capture on an air-bearing testbed
\cite{hovell2021lab}. Crucially, all of this work minimizes base disturbance only during the
pre-grasp \emph{approach} --- before contact dynamics are engaged --- and stops short of the coupled,
post-grasp regime.

\subsection{Contact and detumbling}
Post-grasp detumbling is a thinner, predominantly model-based literature. Active resistance control
(ARC) \cite{activeresist2025} is its strongest representative: a force-feedback contact law that
detumbles a non-cooperative target using only measured contact force and relative motion, with
\emph{no} target mass/inertia identification --- it is therefore inertia-agnostic by design. Two
properties matter here. First, ARC's law contains no term that responds to a disturbance acting on
the \emph{chaser's own base}; it tracks contact force only. Second, it requires a contact-force
(force/torque) sensor. On the RL side, soft-capture work with tactile feedback \cite{softcapture2409}
addresses the instant of contact but does not clearly model free-floating base dynamics or report a
model-based baseline. A recent survey of on-orbit-servicing and active-debris-removal manipulators
\cite{review2024} corroborates this positioning.

\subsection{Positioning}
Against this backdrop, our contribution is the post-contact case that cluster~B stops short of and
cluster~C's model-based law does not address: rejecting a disturbance originating at the chaser's
\emph{own base} \emph{during} contact detumbling, benchmarked against a correctly-characterized ARC
and reported on a space-specific metric (base-attitude disturbance / induced ACS fuel). Unlike
target-side inertia robustness --- which ARC already provides by design --- chaser-side base-attitude
rejection during contact is a genuinely uncontested gap.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\subsection{Free-floating dynamics}
The chaser is a rigid base carrying a planar arm; gravity is zero (orbital free-fall). With no
external wrench, the system's linear and angular momentum are conserved, so arm motion induces a
reaction on the base: accelerating the joints rotates the base in the opposite sense. Writing the
generalized coordinates as the base pose $\mathbf{q}_b\in SE(3)$ and joint angles
$\mathbf{q}_a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$, the coupled equations of motion take the standard manipulator form
$\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}} + \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})\dot{\mathbf{q}}
= \boldsymbol{\tau} + \mathbf{J}_c^{\top}\mathbf{f}_c + \mathbf{w}_{\text{ext}}$, where only the arm
DOFs are actuated ($\boldsymbol{\tau}$), $\mathbf{f}_c$ is the contact wrench mapped through the
contact Jacobian $\mathbf{J}_c$, and $\mathbf{w}_{\text{ext}}$ collects external disturbances on the
base. The off-diagonal (base--arm) blocks of $\mathbf{M}$ are the coupling that makes detumbling on a
free-floating platform fundamentally different from a fixed-base arm. Contact is modeled as a soft,
friction-bearing interaction (MuJoCo's compliant contact model).

\subsection{Simulation scaffold}
We implement the scaffold in MuJoCo (Fig.~\ref{fig:scene}) with zero gravity (orbital free-fall),
a $2$\,ms physics timestep (implicit-fast integrator), and control applied at $100$\,Hz (a
frame-skip of $5$). The chaser is a free-floating base ($256$\,kg, $0.8$\,m box, a 6-DOF free
joint) carrying a planar $3$-DOF arm whose three revolute joints rotate about the out-of-plane
($z$) axis, with capsule links of length $0.5/0.5/0.3$\,m driven by three torque motors saturating
at $\pm 5$\,N\,m. The non-cooperative target is a rigid $0.5$\,m box ($62.5$\,kg nominal, diagonal
inertia $\approx 2.6$\,kg\,m$^2$) on its own free joint, spun up at reset to tumble. Contact uses
MuJoCo's compliant model (friction coefficients $(1, 0.05, 0.001)$, soft-constraint
\texttt{solref} $(0.01, 1)$), so contact forces are continuous rather than rigid-impulsive. The
arm's ready pose places the end-effector a $\approx 0.1$\,m clearance from the target's grasp face,
i.e.\ episodes begin already at the target (post-grasp detumbling scope). Each episode runs at most
$500$ control steps ($5$\,s) and terminates early once the target spin falls below the detumbling
threshold; the policy observes a $32$-D kinematic subset of the full $17/15$ position/velocity state
(the MDP below).

\subsection{Chaser-side base-attitude disturbance}
We model unmodeled chaser actuation noise (ACS/thruster transients) as an external torque
$\mathbf{w}_{\text{ext}}$ applied to the base about the planar detumbling axis, constant in
magnitude over an episode with a random sign, drawn per episode. We report results against the
\emph{open-loop} base spin this torque induces under a do-nothing controller (calibrated to
$\approx 0.175$ rad/s per N\,m at nominal inertia), so the disturbance axis is expressed in the
reviewer-interpretable range $0.05$--$0.12$ rad/s. This is a deliberately simple, controllable proxy
--- not a validated hardware noise spectrum (see Limitations).

\subsection{MDP}
State $s\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$: base pose+twist, joint positions/velocities, target relative
pose and angular velocity, and the end-effector-to-grasp vector --- \emph{kinematics only}, with no
contact-force or target-inertia channel. Action $a\in[-1,1]^3$: normalized joint torques. The reward
penalizes target spin, end-effector distance, induced base angular velocity, and control effort,
with a terminal bonus when the target angular velocity falls below a threshold:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r = -w_\omega\lVert\boldsymbol{\omega}_t\rVert - w_d d_{\text{eg}}
    - w_b\lVert\boldsymbol{\omega}_b\rVert - w_u\lVert a\rVert^2 + r_{\text{term}},
\end{equation}
with $(w_\omega, w_d, w_b, w_u) = (1.0, 0.5, 0.3, 0.01)$ and $r_{\text{term}}=100$. The
$w_b\lVert\boldsymbol{\omega}_b\rVert$ term is the \emph{base-attitude penalty} --- the channel that
drives disturbance rejection; the ablation in Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation} sets $w_b=0$.

\section{Method}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} summarizes the closed-loop setup: a kinematics-only observation drives the SAC
policy, whose torques act on the free-floating environment; an injected chaser-side base disturbance
perturbs the chaser during contact, and the reward of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward} trains the policy to
detumble the target while rejecting that disturbance.

\subsection{Soft Actor-Critic objective}
We train a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) policy --- an off-policy actor-critic for continuous control that
augments the expected return with a policy-entropy bonus, trading exploitation against exploration.
The policy $\pi$ maximizes the maximum-entropy objective
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:sac}
J(\pi) = \sum_{t} \mathbb{E}_{(s_t,a_t)\sim\rho_\pi}\!\left[\, r(s_t,a_t)
         + \alpha\,\mathcal{H}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s_t)\big) \right],
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the detumbling reward of Eq.~\eqref{eq:reward}, $\mathcal{H}$ is the entropy of the
action distribution, and the temperature $\alpha$ is auto-tuned against a target entropy of
$-\dim(\mathcal{A})$. A twin soft Q-critic is trained by minimizing the soft Bellman residual. We use
Stable-Baselines3 with stock SAC hyperparameters and a two-layer $[256,256]$ MLP for both actor and
critic, to keep the result reproducible.

\subsection{Training and domain randomization}
We train for $120$k environment steps per seed and three seeds. Inputs are the $32$-D kinematic
state; outputs are the three normalized joint torques. Following the sim-to-real
dynamics-randomization recipe \cite{domrand}, to induce both inertia robustness and disturbance
rejection we domain-randomize, per episode: the target mass and principal inertia
($0.5\times$--$2\times$ nominal), the initial tumble rate, and the chaser-side base-disturbance
torque (uniform in $[0, 0.8]$ N\,m, covering the evaluated $0.05$--$0.12$ rad/s range with margin).
The base-attitude penalty $w_b$ in the reward is what couples this randomization to a rejection
behavior; we verify that coupling by ablation. Fig.~\ref{fig:learning_curve} shows the training
return: pooled over the three seeds it climbs from $\approx -400$ to a positive plateau as the policy
learns to both detumble the target (earning the terminal bonus) and hold its own base steady.

\section{Baselines}
The headline baseline is \textbf{ARC} (active resistance control) \cite{activeresist2025},
re-implemented from its published control law rather than as a generic impedance controller: a
pre-contact Cartesian reach, then a two-phase force-feedback normal law (an anti-separation phase
followed by incremental contact-force tracking) combined with a tangential motion controller that
opposes the contact-point surface velocity to bleed the target's spin. The law uses only measured
contact force and relative motion --- no target mass or inertia --- and therefore requires a
contact-force sensor. Gains are tuned to our simulation scaffold.

Concretely, before contact a Cartesian PD reaches the grasp point,
$\mathbf{f}_{\text{ee}} = k_p(\mathbf{x}_g - \mathbf{x}_{\text{ee}}) - k_d\dot{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{ee}}$.
On contact, the ARC normal-force magnitude follows the published two-phase law: an anti-separation
phase $u = \beta_1\max_t F_c$ for the first $T_s$ contact steps, then incremental tracking
$u \leftarrow u + \beta_2 (F^\star - F_c)$ (clamped to $[0, u_{\max}]$), applied along the inward
contact normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$. A tangential controller opposes the contact-point surface velocity
$\mathbf{v}_{\text{surf}} = \boldsymbol{\omega}_t \times \mathbf{r}$ to bleed the target's spin, and
joint torques follow from the end-effector Jacobian transpose:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:arc}
\mathbf{f}_{\text{ee}} = u\,\hat{\mathbf{n}} - k_r\,\mathbf{v}_{\text{tan}}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{J}_{\text{ee}}^{\top}\mathbf{f}_{\text{ee}} - k_v\,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_a,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_{\text{tan}}$ is the component of $\mathbf{v}_{\text{surf}}$ orthogonal to
$\hat{\mathbf{n}}$. Every quantity is measured ($F_c$, $\boldsymbol{\omega}_t$, end-effector
kinematics); no target inertia enters, so ARC is inertia-agnostic by construction. We use
$F^\star=20$\,N, $\beta_1=\beta_2=0.5$, $T_s=10$ steps, $k_p=40$, $k_d=8$, and $k_r=8$.
We also include a \textbf{zero} controller (no torque) as a do-nothing reference, whose induced base
spin equals the injected open-loop disturbance and whose target spin is essentially unchanged.

\section{Experiments}

We evaluate the learned policy (SAC, $120$k steps, $3$ seeds, pooled) against ARC and a zero-control
reference on paired sweeps: every controller sees the same episodes, defined by target inertia scale,
injected base-disturbance torque, initial spin magnitude $\{0.2, 0.3, 0.4\}$ rad/s, and seed
$\{0,\dots,4\}$. Each episode runs to detumbling (target spin below $0.05$ rad/s) or $500$ steps. We
report peak base-attitude disturbance (induced base angular velocity; proxy for chaser ACS fuel),
spin reduction, and success rate, with bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals ($10$k resamples).

\subsection{Headline: base-attitude disturbance rejection}

Fig.~\ref{fig:base_disturbance} and Table~\ref{tab:base_disturbance} report peak base-attitude
disturbance as the injected chaser-side disturbance grows, at nominal target inertia. The learned
policy holds the base nearly steady --- \textbf{0.023--0.025 rad/s}, essentially flat across the
$0.05$--$0.12$ rad/s range and \emph{below} the open-loop (zero-control) level --- whereas ARC sits
at \textbf{0.123--0.128 rad/s} regardless of the injection, because its force-tracking law carries no
term that responds to base attitude. This is a \textbf{$\sim 80\%$ reduction} ($\approx 5\times$) in
the chaser-attitude cost, at \emph{matched} detumbling: both controllers reduce target spin
comparably ($85\%$ RL vs.\ $82\%$ ARC) with $100\%$ success at nominal inertia.

\subsection{Inertia parity}

With no injected disturbance, the inertia sweep (Table~\ref{tab:parity} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:inertia}) is a fairness check, not a failure claim: ARC is inertia-agnostic by design, and indeed both controllers maintain detumbling
with comparable spin reduction (RL $83$--$88\%$, ARC $74$--$83\%$). Two points stand out. First,
ARC's \emph{contact-induced} base disturbance already grows steeply with target inertia
($0.051\!\to\!0.294$ rad/s) before any injected disturbance, while the learned policy stays low
($0.018\!\to\!0.046$); the chaser-attitude advantage is therefore not an artifact of the injection.
Second, ARC's success rate falls to $67\%$ at $1.5$--$2\times$ inertia; we attribute this to detumble
\emph{speed} under the fixed $500$-step horizon and our scaffold-specific gain tuning rather than a
fundamental inertia limitation (its spin-reduction stays high), consistent with ARC's design claim of
inertia-agnosticism. We treat spin-reduction parity, not the threshold-crossing count, as the
relevant parity metric.

\subsection{The rejection gap grows with target mass}

At a fixed mid-range disturbance ($0.5$ N\,m, $\approx 0.09$ rad/s), the advantage widens with target
mass (Table~\ref{tab:gapmass}): the ARC-to-RL ratio of peak base disturbance grows from $2.8\times$ at
$0.5\times$ inertia to $6.4\times$ at $2\times$. Heavier targets dump more momentum into the chaser
through contact; ARC's force-tracking transmits it to the base, the learned policy absorbs it.

\subsection{Ablation: the base-attitude penalty}
\label{sec:ablation}

Removing the base-attitude penalty from the reward ($w_b=0$, retrained with the same disturbance
randomization) degrades rejection from $0.023$ to $0.035$ rad/s ($\sim 50\%$ worse) --- but does
\emph{not} collapse it to ARC's level ($\sim 0.125$). Two mechanisms therefore contribute: the
explicit penalty sharpens rejection, and, independently, the free-floating RL formulation itself
learns a momentum-aware detumbling strategy that is inherently gentler on the base than ARC's pure
force-tracking. We report the penalty as a contributing, not sole, cause rather than overclaim it.

\subsection{Sensing}

The learned policy observes \emph{kinematics only} (the $32$-D state; no contact-force or inertia
channel), whereas ARC's control law requires a measured contact force. The policy nonetheless matches
ARC's detumbling and beats it on base disturbance --- a sensing-reduction result: comparable task
performance with strictly less instrumentation.

\section{Limitations and Sim-to-Real}
Our scaffold is deliberately minimal and the results should be read with its limits in mind. (i) The
arm is a planar $3$-DOF mechanism and the target has diagonal inertia; a full $6$-DOF arm with
products of inertia would exercise out-of-plane coupling we do not test. (ii) The disturbance is a
constant-magnitude base torque --- a controllable proxy for ACS/thruster noise, \emph{not} a
validated hardware noise spectrum; real disturbances are time-varying and colored. (iii) Contact uses
MuJoCo's compliant model; contact-rich sim-to-real for grasping is itself an open problem. (iv) We
report no hardware experiments: deep-RL-guided capture has been demonstrated on air-bearing hardware
for the \emph{pre-contact} phase \cite{hovell2021lab}, but contact-rich detumbling on a free-floating
base has not, and the compliant-contact and free-floating coupling we rely on are precisely the parts
most sensitive to the sim-to-real gap. (v) ARC is re-implemented and gain-tuned to our scaffold, so
its absolute numbers (e.g.\ the high-inertia success drop) reflect our tuning, not a definitive bound
on the published method; we therefore lean on spin-reduction parity rather than the success count for
the inertia comparison. Future work: a higher-fidelity $6$-DOF arm with products of inertia, realistic
time-varying (colored) disturbance spectra calibrated to a measured ACS/thruster profile, and
hardware-in-the-loop validation of the contact phase.

\section{Conclusion}
We framed and demonstrated the rejection of a \emph{chaser-side} base-attitude disturbance during
contact detumbling of a tumbling, non-cooperative target by a free-floating space manipulator --- a
gap left open by pre-contact RL (which minimizes base disturbance only before contact) and by
model-based force-tracking such as ARC (which carries no base-attitude term). A SAC policy observing
kinematics only holds peak base disturbance roughly $5\times$ lower than ARC across the tested
disturbance range, with the gap widening to $6.4\times$ at $2\times$ nominal inertia, at matched
detumbling and confirmed inertia parity. Because base-attitude disturbance maps directly to chaser
ACS fuel, this is a mission-relevant advantage for on-orbit servicing, achieved with strictly less
sensing than the model-based baseline.
