**Sustainable Space Systems & Orbital Lifecycle**

Detailed Participant Guide

```
Space systems can no longer be evaluated only by what they achieve during active
mission operations. A responsible mission concept must also consider how it is
designed, how efficiently it uses orbital resources, and what happens when the mission
ends.
This challenge asks participants to propose a space mission concept or a space
subsystem that embeds sustainability across the full orbital lifecycle, from mission
design through operations to end-of-life disposal, deorbiting, reuse, or servicing.
Participants may propose a full mission architecture or a focused subsystem such
as a deorbiting mechanism, propulsion-optimization concept, debris-avoidance
strategy, reusable platform idea, or another technically relevant contribution to
long-term orbital sustainability.
The strongest projects combine realistic mission assumptions, measurable
sustainability indicators, and simulation or analysis that demonstrates why the
concept is better than a conventional or poorly planned alternative.
```
**1. Scope and Topic**


Create a validated space mission or subsystem concept that explicitly addresses
sustainability throughout the orbital lifecycle. Your project must show not only what the
system does during the mission, but also how it minimizes long-term orbital impact
through measurable planning, engineering choices, and end-of-life strategy.
The project must define clear sustainability KPIs such as post-mission orbital
lifetime, probability of successful deorbit, debris-risk reduction, fuel efficiency, or
another well-justified metric tied directly to the selected concept.
The sustainability claim must be supported by analysis, calculations, simulation
outputs, or structured comparison against a baseline case.

**3. Challenge Goal**

```
IEarth orbits are increasingly congested. Missions that ignore debris mitigation, fuel
efficiency, collision risk, and end-of-life planning may create long-term hazards that
outlast the useful life of the spacecraft itself.
Even technically successful missions can become sustainability failures if they
leave inactive objects in orbit, rely on unrealistic propellant assumptions, or operate
without a credible disposal strategy.
The challenge is therefore not simply to design something that can fly. The
challenge is to design something that can operate responsibly and leave the
orbital environment in a safer condition than a careless alternative would.
```
**2. Problem Statement**


A mission concept or subsystem concept connected to one clearly defined
sustainability problem in space operations.
A lifecycle-based engineering narrative covering design assumptions, orbit
selection, operational behavior, and end-of-life outcomes.
A simulation, tool-based study, or technical analysis showing why the concept is
feasible and how it improves sustainability metrics.
A measurable sustainability framework using KPIs, timeline reasoning, and
defendable assumptions.

**4. What Participants Are Expected to Build**

```
Earth-observation mission with a built-in post-mission disposal strategy
Climate-monitoring spacecraft with propellant-aware operations
Drag-sail or passive-deorbit subsystem for small satellites
Reusable or serviceable spacecraft architecture
Autonomous debris-avoidance or collision-risk reduction concept
```
**Example application directions**

```
5.1 Define the Mission or Subsystem Clearly
Choose one mission concept or one subsystem focus area.
State the mission purpose, the sustainability problem being addressed, and why
space is the appropriate domain.
If proposing a subsystem rather than a full mission, explain where it fits within a
larger spacecraft architecture.
Keep the concept focused; depth and lifecycle clarity are more valuable than
broad but shallow ambition.
```
**5. Technical Instructions**


**5.2 Define the Operational and Orbital Context**

```
State the assumed orbit class conceptually, such as LEO, MEO, or GEO, and explain
why that choice matches the mission objective.
Describe expected mission duration, operational behavior, and key resource
constraints such as mass, power, propulsion, or maneuver budget.
Explain the main lifecycle phases: design assumptions, launch insertion context,
active operations, and post-mission disposition.
Use realistic orbital assumptions appropriate for a student-level concept study.
```
**5. Technical Instructions (Cont.)**

**5.3 Define Sustainability KPIs**

```
Select measurable indicators that allow a judge to evaluate whether your concept
is truly more sustainable.
Examples include post-mission orbital lifetime, debris generation risk, deorbit time,
fuel efficiency, collision-risk reduction, successful-disposal probability, servicing
potential, or reuse potential.
Each KPI should be tied directly to a design choice rather than listed as a generic
aspiration.
Where exact values are not possible, provide justified approximations, ranges, or
comparative metrics.
```
**5.4 Design the End-of-Life and Debris-Mitigation Strategy**

```
Explain clearly what happens when the mission ends.
State whether the spacecraft is deorbited, passively disposed of, moved to a
graveyard orbit, serviced, reused, or otherwise managed responsibly.
Discuss how long disposal takes, how collision risk is reduced, and what
assumptions are required for success.
No concept should be treated as sustainable unless the post-mission path is
explicit and technically credible.
```

```
5.5 Demonstrate Technical Feasibility with Analysis or
Simulation
Use a simulation tool, orbital analysis workflow, spreadsheet model, or structured
engineering calculation to support your concept.
Show outputs such as orbit lifetime estimation, deorbit timeline, maneuver
comparison, propellant-use comparison, drag-effect analysis, collision-avoidance
logic, or another technically relevant result.
Include a baseline case and an improved case whenever possible so judges can
see what changed and why it matters.
The analysis does not need to be industrial-grade, but it must be defendable,
organized, and relevant to the selected concept.
```
**5. Technical Instructions (Cont.)**

**5.6 Keep Assumptions Realistic and Explainable**

```
Avoid overly speculative architectures that cannot be justified technically.
Do not claim debris mitigation, servicing, or disposal success without explaining the
underlying mechanism.
Keep the project within the level of detail that can be explained clearly during
judging.
Use a level of complexity appropriate for a student/YP hackathon submission rather
than a full mission design review.
```

**12. Tips for Strong Submissions**

```
Address sustainability from the very start of the concept rather than adding it at the
end.
Use clear lifecycle diagrams, disposal logic, or mission timelines.
Define a small number of strong KPIs instead of many weak ones.
Compare a baseline case to an improved case whenever possible.
Keep orbital assumptions realistic and clearly stated.
```
**13. Common Mistakes to Avoid**

```
No end-of-life plan or a vague disposal statement
Claiming sustainability without measurable KPIs
Using unrealistic orbit, fuel, or mission assumptions
Providing no simulation or analysis evidence
Treating sustainability as a side note rather than the core design logic
```
**14. Final Advice to Participants**

Ask yourself: “What happens to this system after the mission ends — and how does that
outcome affect space 10, 20, or 50 years from now?” If your submission answers that
clearly, quantitatively, and credibly, then it is well aligned with Challenge 4.
